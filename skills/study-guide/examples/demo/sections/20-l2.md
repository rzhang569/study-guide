## Conditional probability and Bayes' rule

::: must If this lecture feels abstract, the exam only needs these three moves
1. Condition = shrink the world to what you know, then measure inside it.
2. Independent means knowing one event does not change the other's probability.
3. Bayes: work out both branches that lead to the evidence, then take the share of the branch you care about.
:::

::: terms
- conditional probability: $P(A \mid B)$: probability of A once you know B happened
- independent: $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$; equivalently $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$
- law of total probability: split by cases, weight each case by how common it is, add
- prior / posterior: belief before / after seeing the evidence
- base rate: how common the condition is in the population (the prior)
:::

::: terms Everyday analogies
- Conditioning on B: Only looking at the rows of a spreadsheet where column B says "yes"
- Base rate: How many needles there are in the haystack before you start searching
- False positive: A smoke alarm going off for toast
:::

### 2.1 Conditional probability {#l2-cond p3}
src: L2 p.4–8
lead: Knowing B happened shrinks the sample space to B; $P(A \mid B)$ is the share of B that is also A.

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0$$

::: eg
One fair die. Given the roll is even, the world is {2, 4, 6}; one of those is a 6, so P(6 | even) = 1/3 (L2 p.5).
:::

::: plain
Filter first, then count. Out of the even rolls, what fraction are sixes?
:::

### 2.2 Independence {#l2-indep p2}
src: L2 p.10–14
lead: A and B are independent when learning one tells you nothing about the other.

::: trap
Disjoint is not independent. If A and B cannot both happen and each has positive probability, learning A tells you B did **not** happen: $P(B \mid A) = 0 \ne P(B)$. That is strong dependence.
:::

::: eg Check it with numbers
Two fair coin flips. A = "first is H", M = "the two flips match". P(M) = 1/2 and P(M | A) = P(second is H) = 1/2, so A and M are independent even though both mention the first flip (L2 p.13).
:::

### 2.3 Total probability and Bayes' rule {#l2-bayes p3}
src: L2 p.18–30 · practice sheet Q4–Q5
lead: To reverse a conditional, count how often the evidence shows up on each branch, then take the share that comes from the branch you care about.

$$P(D\mid +)=\frac{P(+\mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+\mid D)\,P(D)+P(+\mid D^c)\,P(D^c)}$$

::: figure tree
1000 people
- 10 sick (1%)
  - 9 test + {hl}
  - 1 test − {ghost}
- 990 healthy (99%)
  - ≈50 test + {wn}
  - 940 test − {ghost}
caption: Disease in 1% of people, test catches 90% of cases, 5% false positives. Positives: 9 + 50 = 59, and only 9 of them are sick, so P(sick | +) = 9/59 ≈ 15% (L2 p.24).
:::

::: plain
The test is good, but healthy people outnumber sick people 99 to 1. Even a small false-alarm rate on that huge group produces more positives than the real cases do.
:::

::: trap
$P(+ \mid D)$ (how good the test is) is not $P(D \mid +)$ (what a positive means for you). Mixing them up is called base-rate neglect.
:::
