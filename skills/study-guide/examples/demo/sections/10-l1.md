## Counting and probability basics

::: terms
- sample space $\Omega$: the list of every possible outcome of the experiment
- event: a set of outcomes, e.g. "the sum is 7"
- complement $A^c$: everything in $\Omega$ that is not in $A$
- union / intersection: $A \cup B$: A or B (or both). $A \cap B$: both at once
- permutation: an ordered arrangement: first, second, third…
- combination: an unordered selection: who is in, not in what order
:::

### 1.1 Equally likely outcomes {#l1-equally p3}
src: L1 p.6–9
lead: When every outcome is equally likely, a probability is a count: favorable outcomes divided by all outcomes.

$$P(A)=\frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}$$ only when every outcome in Ω is equally likely

::: figure grid
rows: 6
cols: 6
top: die 1
left: die 2
col-heads: 1..6
row-heads: 1..6
hl: r + c = 7
wn: r + c = 2
- ttl: Sum = 7: 6 of 36 cells
- acc: P = 6/36 = 1/6
-
- ttl: Sum = 2: only (1, 1)
- warn: P = 1/36
-
- small: The 36 ordered pairs are equally likely;
- small: the 11 sums are not.
caption: Two fair dice. Count cells, not sums: a sum of 7 has six cells, a sum of 2 has one (L1 p.8).
:::

::: plain
Write out every outcome so that each one is equally likely, then count. For two dice the right list is the 36 ordered pairs (die 1, die 2), not the 11 sums.
:::

::: trap
"There are 11 possible sums, so P(sum = 7) = 1/11" is wrong: the sums are not equally likely. Always check that your outcomes are.
:::

### 1.2 Complement and inclusion–exclusion {#l1-rules p3}
src: L1 p.12–16
lead: "At least one" is easiest as one minus "none"; for "A or B", add both and subtract the overlap once.

$$P(A^c)=1-P(A)$$
$$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$$ the overlap was counted twice

::: eg
Roll a fair die 4 times. P(at least one six) = 1 − P(no six) = $1-(5/6)^4$ = 1 − 625/1296 ≈ 0.518 (L1 p.14).
:::

::: trap
$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$ only when A and B cannot happen together. Otherwise you count the overlap twice and can even get a "probability" above 1.
:::

### 1.3 Permutations and combinations {#l1-count p2}
src: L1 p.18–24
lead: Ask "does order matter?" If yes, multiply the choices; if no, divide out the orderings.

| Question | Order? | Count from 8 people |
|---|---|---|
| President, VP, treasurer | yes | 8 · 7 · 6 = 336 |
| A 3-person committee | no | $\binom{8}{3}=\frac{336}{3!}=56$ |

::: plain
Every committee of 3 can be turned into 3! = 6 different officer lists by handing out the titles. So officer lists are 6 times as many as committees.
:::

::: trap
Repeated letters: BOOK has 4!/2! = 12 arrangements, not 24, because swapping the two O's changes nothing.
:::
